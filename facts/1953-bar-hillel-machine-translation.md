## What happened
In the July 1953 issue of *Philosophy of Science* (Vol. 20, No. 3, pp. 217–225), philosopher and linguist Yehoshua Bar-Hillel—drawing on his work as the first full-time machine translation researcher at MIT's Research Laboratory of Electronics—analyzed the obstacles facing automated translation without human understanding. He centered his critique on four problems: operational syntax (explicit, step-by-step grammatical rules a machine can apply mechanically), differences in structure between languages, idioms whose meanings cannot be built word by word, and the search for universal grammatical categories. Concluding that fully automatic high-quality translation remained out of reach, he advocated practical human-machine workflows using human pre-editors or post-editors to refine machine output.

## Why it matters
Bar-Hillel's sober assessment tempered early 1950s optimism about word-for-word translation and laid conceptual foundations for both formal computational syntax and computer-aided translation.
